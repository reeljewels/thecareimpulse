Strengthening the Social Security Caregiver Credit

Recommendations for the next version of the Social Security Caregiver Credit Act, from a long-term family caregiver

Ginny Lee Sayre · Cupertino, California · November 2026

A caregiver credit should recognize care freely given, not reward caregivers for being unable to stop.

Summary

The Social Security Caregiver Credit Act would, for the first time, let unpaid family caregivers earn Social Security credit for the work they do. That principle is right. But as written, the credit would miss most long-term caregivers, reward exactly the overwork that burns them out, and leave verification undefined. Because the current bill will expire when the 119th Congress ends in January 2027, its reintroduction is the moment to fix these problems.

The stakes are large. AARP’s Valuing the Invaluable 2026 estimates that 59 million family caregivers of adults provided 49.5 billion hours of care in 2024, worth $1.01 trillion, more than all Medicaid spending that year. Caregiving in the U.S. 2025 found that one-third of caregivers have been caring for five years or more, which is exactly the group a five-year cap would shortchange. And AARP’s new report, The Hidden Cost of Caregiving, shows how long the cost can last: in one modeled example, a woman who leaves work for ten years to give care collects about 20 percent less in Social Security every month for the rest of her life.

I have been my mother’s primary caregiver for seventeen years, since I was twenty-seven. Under the bill as drafted, at most five of those years could count, and possibly none. The recommendations below come from that experience and from a free tool I built, the Caregiver Scope and Consent Record, which records what a family caregiver is open to doing, what they aren’t taking on, and when that changes.

What the current bill does

S. 4396 was introduced by Sen. Chris Murphy on April 27, 2026, with Sen. Kirsten Gillibrand, and referred to the Senate Finance Committee. Rep. Brad Schneider introduced the House companion, H.R. 8490. As introduced, it would:

  • Treat months of unpaid care as deemed wages for Social Security, counting only the last 60 qualifying months (five years).
  • Require at least 80 hours of care in a month for that month to qualify.
  • Cover care for a dependent relative under age 12 or a chronically dependent individual who needs daily help with activities of daily living or instrumental activities of daily living, such as communication, finances, shopping, household chores, and travel in the community.
  • Require an application, physician documentation of the relative’s dependency, and verification rules to be set by the Social Security Administration.

Public summaries differ on whether caregiving before enactment would count. That ambiguity should be resolved explicitly in the next version.

1. Don’t make reducing care costly. This is the most important change. A credit should recognize care freely given, not create a financial reason to keep giving more than a caregiver can sustain. Under a single 80-hour threshold, a caregiver who responsibly reduces her care from 100 hours to 60 a month, perhaps by bringing in paid help or letting the care recipient do more for themselves, simply stops earning credit. Months already credited stay credited, but going forward the threshold rewards exactly the overwork that burns caregivers out. Graduated tiers would let credit follow the care actually given, so setting a boundary costs a little, not everything.

2. Credit past caregiving, explicitly. The people a caregiver credit is meant to help have often already given years. A credit that starts only after enactment tells a caregiver seventeen years in that her work so far doesn’t count. If past months are eligible, the bill should say so plainly and set out how they can be documented.

3. Lift or scale the five-year cap. Many family caregivers serve far longer than five years. Every year out of paid work is a zero year in a Social Security benefit calculation, and a five-year cap leaves most of a long-term caregiver’s history unrecognized.

4. Count the care that happens off the bedside. Much of caregiving is active work done away from the patient: calls with agencies, scheduling, insurance and pharmacy work, and portal messages; researching a diagnosis, treatment options, or medication interactions; and consulting with clinicians outside of appointments, whether preparing questions, following up on results, or seeking a second opinion. These are measurable tasks and should count toward qualifying hours. Standby time is different. Being available for an emergency restricts a caregiver’s freedom and earnings without being the same as active care. Rather than counting every available hour, Congress should direct SSA to study how documented on-call restrictions could be recognized.

5. Recognize skilled medical care. The bill’s dependency test covers activities of daily living and some instrumental activities, including communication and finances. It does not explicitly address specialized clinical caregiving, such as managing an infusion pump, oxygen equipment, or complex medications, or say how those responsibilities satisfy the dependency test. Some caregivers do exactly this work for someone who can still dress and feed themselves. The definition should account for skilled medical tasks.

Eligibility and calculation are separate questions. Who qualifies for the credit and how much credit a month is worth should be decided separately. Sixty hours of skilled infusion management and sixty hours of routine help may both deserve recognition without being valued identically, or they may be valued the same. Either way, Congress should state explicitly whether hours, care intensity, lost earnings, or a combination determine the credit’s value, rather than letting a single hours threshold decide both who qualifies and what they receive.

A documentation model

Every caregiver credit has to answer one question: how do you know the care happened? Three pieces together could answer it without turning caregivers’ lives into an audit:

  1. A scope and consent record, stating what the caregiver is open to doing, what they aren’t taking on, their maximum hours, and when the record expires. This documents care going forward and prevents past help from being treated as an open-ended obligation.
  2. A simple care log of hours and tasks, kept by the caregiver.
  3. Existing records as corroboration, with the care recipient’s authorization: visit notes naming the caregiver, patient-portal proxy activity, and specialty pharmacy training records. These can also support credit for years of care that happened before any form existed.

The Caregiver Scope and Consent Record already exists as a free, working tool, and could be adapted for SSA use.

Two design principles matter here. First, the consent record and the care log do different jobs: one records willingness and limits, the other records care actually given. A caregiver might document 200 hours of past care while declining every future task, and the system should preserve both facts. Second, verification cannot depend solely on the care recipient’s agreement. Recipients may have cognitive impairment, may dispute hours, or may be the very people who benefited from the unpaid work. Caregiver attestation, records-based corroboration, and professional review together can guard against fraud without giving any one party the power to erase legitimate care.

An alternative pathway is also needed when the care recipient cannot or will not authorize access to their records, whether because of cognitive impairment, death, estrangement, or refusal. In those cases, the caregiver’s attestation could be supported by other evidence: a clinician’s statement about the caregiver’s role, home care agency or pharmacy records naming the caregiver, contemporaneous calendars and correspondence, and statements from third parties, all subject to SSA review.

Why now

S. 4396 has not moved since its referral to the Finance Committee and will expire at the end of the 119th Congress. Any caregiver credit in the next Congress will need to be reintroduced. AARP’s new research on the lifetime cost of caregiving, and the start of a new Congress, make this the moment to write a credit that reaches the caregivers it is meant for.
